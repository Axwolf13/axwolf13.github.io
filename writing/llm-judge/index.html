I built an LLM judge to check my own scorer. It disagreed with me.

I wrote a deterministic index that scores how hard a Terms of Service document works to hide that "Buy now" means "revocable licence". Then I had two language models read the same documents and score them independently. I expected a nice validation number. I got a correlation of 0.11.

That is roughly the correlation between two things that have nothing to do with each other.

My first instinct was that I had a bug. I didn't. The disagreement is real, it survives a much bigger model, and it turned out to be the most useful thing the whole experiment produced. Here is what happened and what I actually believe now.

The setup

DODI is a keyword scorer. It counts licence words against ownership words, penalises unreadable prose, counts red-flag clauses, and weights those 50/25/25 into a 0 to 100 score. Same document in, same score out, no model involved. That determinism is the point: it's what makes a 2015-to-2024 comparison honest.

The judge is the opposite kind of instrument. Each document goes to a language model with a rubric that mirrors DODI's three components, and the model returns structured JSON: subscores, an overall deception score, a letter grade and a short rationale. Every response is cached to disk, so the analysis reruns without touching a model and anyone can check my numbers without paying for tokens.

Two judges, deliberately different sizes. Claude Haiku, small and fast, on all 52 documents. Claude Sonnet, considerably stronger, on the 12 documents that also have human expert grades from ToS;DR. Both ran locally through the Claude CLI on my normal subscription, so this cost me nothing but time.

Before looking at agreement I checked the judge was stable at all. Ten documents, three runs each: mean per-document standard deviation of 6.3 points, worst spread 20. Not noise-free, but nowhere near enough noise to explain what came next.

Spearman rank correlations. Three things fall out of that table, and I trust them in this order.

One: the disagreement is not a small-model artifact. This was my main worry. Cheap model, sloppy scores, case closed. So I ran a much stronger model over the validation set and it landed at 0.10, essentially identical. The two models even fail differently. Haiku scores high and narrow, handing out a lot of 68s. Sonnet scores lower and wider, putting most mainstream contracts at "moderate". Different distributions, same verdict: neither of them reads these documents the way DODI counts them.

Two: the readers agree with each other more than either agrees with me. Sonnet and Haiku correlate at 0.44 with each other, against roughly 0.10 with DODI. There is a rough LLM consensus about these documents and my index is not part of it.

Three, and this is the part I did not expect: DODI still tracks the humans best. Against ToS;DR's expert grades, my word-counter scores 0.54, Sonnet 0.46, Haiku 0.30. The dumb instrument matches the humans better than the smart ones do.

So this is not a story about the LLM being right and my scorer being wrong. It's messier and more interesting than that.

Adobe is where you can see it

The single clearest disagreement in the corpus is Adobe's 2024 terms. DODI gives it 95.7, the most deceptive document in the entire study. Sonnet gives it 20. Haiku gives it 44. When something is that far apart, one of the two instruments is measuring the wrong thing.

Both models pointed at the same reason. Adobe states plainly that its software is licensed and not sold. It carries plain-language summaries. It says it won't train AI on your content. And critically, Adobe never dangles a "Buy" button and then hands you a licence, because everybody already knows Adobe is a subscription.

DODI cannot see any of that. It sees a document stuffed with the word "licence" and almost no "buy" or "own", so the ratio metric maxes out and the score goes to 96. My index conflates licensing-language density with deception. An honest subscription service that never pretends you're buying anything scores as maximally deceptive. That is a false positive, and it took a reader to find it, because a counter cannot find it by construction.

Which points straight at the fix, and it's one I'd already written into the repo's future work without realising it was load-bearing. Ownership deception is not "how much licence language is there". It's the gap between what the storefront promises and what the contract grants. High only when the button says Buy and the fine print says licence. Under that definition Adobe drops to honest and Netflix stays exactly where it is. That's DODI v2 and it exists because the judge disagreed with v1.

The judge also caught something I got wrong

Separately, and slightly embarrassingly: one document in my corpus was mislabeled. I had a file called whatsapp_tos.txt. It was Wikipedia's Terms of Use. Forty-three mentions of "Wikimedia", zero mentions of "WhatsApp".

The grade and the service ID attached to it were both correct and both Wikipedia's, so the correlation was never affected. Only the name on the file was wrong. My deterministic pipeline had scored that document happily for weeks, because it doesn't read, it counts, and a keyword counter has no opinion about whether a document is what you claim it is.

The judge opened it, scored it a 6 out of 100, and explained that it governs community-contributed content under copyleft licences. Not a messaging app. The mismatch was obvious the second something actually read the text.

That is the argument for a second instrument in one paragraph. The deterministic scorer is reproducible and blind. The reader is fallible and awake. You want both, and you especially want them when they disagree.

What I actually believe now

Three defensible methods for measuring the same construct barely converge. A keyword index, two language models and a panel of human volunteers produce four different rankings of the same twelve contracts. The index that agrees most with humans is the one that gets Adobe spectacularly wrong.

The honest conclusion is not "use the LLM" or "use the counter". It's that ownership deception is not one number, and any single automated ToS score, including mine, should be read as one instrument's opinion rather than a measurement. I would rather publish that than a tidy validation plot.

Where this is weak

  • Twelve documents. Every human comparison in that table is n=12 and not one of them is statistically significant (p = 0.07, 0.13, 0.34). DODI beating Sonnet against the humans is a near-tie, not a result. Only the Haiku-vs-DODI number rests on the full 52.
  • Uncontrolled temperature. The judges run through the CLI, so I can't pin sampling. The three-run consistency check measures the judge as deployed, not the judge at temperature zero. Defensible, but it isn't the cleaner experiment.
  • One vendor. Two Claude models are not two independent opinions. A cross-vendor judge is the obvious next check and it's queued.
  • The rubric is mine. I wrote both the index and the prompt that grades it. Some shared bias between them is likely, which if anything makes a 0.11 correlation stranger, not safer.

Everything reproduces from the repo. The cached judge responses are committed, so you can rerun the whole agreement analysis without a model or an API key and check whether I read my own numbers correctly.
